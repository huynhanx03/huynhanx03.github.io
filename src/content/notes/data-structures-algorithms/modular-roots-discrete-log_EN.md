Computing `3^5 mod 7` takes only a few squarings. Given the result 5, however, we cannot recover the exponent by taking a real logarithm and rounding. Likewise, a residue can have two modular square roots or none. Inversion depends on the structure of residues rather than the order of real numbers.

We use an odd prime p and values normalized into [0,p), assuming modular exponentiation and inverses are familiar. These are algorithms for study; implementations for large numbers must also prevent multiplication overflow. This lesson is not a cryptographic library design.

## Establish that a square root exists before finding one

Modulo 7, the squares of 0..6 are `0,1,4,2,2,4,1`. Thus 2 has roots 3 and 4, whereas 3 has none. The roots need not be near the real square root of 2. For nonzero a and odd p, if x²=a then (p−x)²=a, giving two distinct roots. Also, `(x−y)(x+y)=0 mod p` shows that every root is x or −x.

Euler's criterion distinguishes the cases: for a≠0, `a^((p−1)/2)` is 1 for a quadratic residue and −1 otherwise. Compute this first to avoid searching for a nonexistent root. Handle a=0, whose root is 0, separately; p=2 also has a tiny domain requiring its own case.

### A shortcut when p is 3 modulo 4

If p≡3 modulo 4 and nonzero a is a residue, set `x=a^((p+1)/4)`. Squaring gives `x²=a·a^((p−1)/2)≡a` by Euler's criterion. For p=7,a=2, the exponent is 2, giving x=4; the other root is 3.

The exponent is an integer for the stated class of primes. Verify x² mod p equals a; this can expose invalid assumptions or arithmetic errors, but does not replace correct multiplication. Primes p≡1 modulo 4 need a more general method.

## Tonelli–Shanks reduces the order of the remaining discrepancy

Write `p−1=q·2^s` with odd q. Choose a non-residue z using Euler's criterion and initialize `r=a^((q+1)/2)`, `t=a^q`, `c=z^q`, `M=s`. Maintain `r²≡a·t`: once t=1, r is a root. The remaining work lies in a group of power-of-two order.

If t≠1, find the smallest i in 1..M−1 satisfying `t^(2^i)=1`. Set `b=c^(2^(M−i−1))`, then update `r←rb`, `t←tb²`, `c←b²`, and `M←i`, modulo p. Both sides of the invariant are multiplied by b². The choice of b reduces the remaining order of t, reflected by the strict decrease in M. Under the stated conditions, the process terminates at t=1.

Also maintain `ord(c)=2^M` and `ord(t)` dividing `2^(M−1)`, ensuring i exists when t≠1. Initially Euler's criterion establishes these for z and a. Both t and b² then have order `2^i`: each raised to `2^(i−1)` is −1, so their product yields 1. The updated t therefore has order below `2^i`, matching the new M=i. [MIT's Tonelli notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-781-theory-of-numbers-spring-2012/resources/mit18_781s12_lec11/) explain this order reduction.

For p=13,a=10, p−1=3·4, so q=3,s=2. Choose non-residue z=2. Then r=10²≡9, t=10³≡12, c=2³≡8. Since t²=1, i=1 and b=8. Updating gives r=9·8≡7 and t=12·8²≡1. Indeed 7²≡10; the other root is 6.

Searching for i by repeated squaring takes O(log p+s²) modular multiplications after finding z; as s≤log₂p, O(log²p) is a common bound for this part. Finding a non-residue is separate: a uniformly sampled nonzero residue succeeds with probability 1/2 per trial, but an arbitrary sequential search has no such small deterministic bound. Complete code must cover a=0,p=2, failure to have a root, numeric bounds, and safe multiplication.

## Discrete logarithms: meet at a shared value

Now solve `g^x≡h (mod p)` for nonzero g. Its powers repeat after ord(g), the order of g. There is no solution if h is outside the subgroup generated by g; h=0 cannot be a power of nonzero g in this multiplicative group either.

Choose `m=ceil(sqrt(ord(g)))`. Every x in [0,ord(g)) has the form `x=im+j`, 0≤j<m. Rewrite the equation as `g^j ≡ h·g^(−im)`. Baby-step giant-step stores g^j, then checks h, h·g^(−m), h·g^(−2m), and so on for a match.

For p=7,g=3,h=5, the order is 6 and m=3. Baby steps are `1→0,3→1,2→2`. The first giant value 5 is absent. Since 3³≡6 and 6 is its own inverse, the next value is 5·6≡2, matching j=2. Thus x=1·3+2=5; verify 3⁵≡5.

With average O(1) hash-table lookup, expected time and memory are O(√ord(g)), apart from initial exponentiation/inversion. Using p−1 as a bound still covers a solution if one exists but may cost more than using the actual order. To obtain the smallest exponent, retain the smallest j for each value and examine/update valid candidates rather than overwriting with the last occurrence.

Verify every candidate with PowMod and the required exponent range; a small order makes several exponents equivalent. Near the 64-bit limit, √p can still require billions of entries. A square-root bound is not automatically practical. The difficulty of discrete logarithms in appropriately chosen groups matters to cryptography, but safe groups and parameters are a separate subject.

## Try these first

1. List the quadratic residues modulo 7 and the roots of 2.
2. Solve `3^x≡5 (mod 7)` with BSGS and m=3.
3. Why is `2^x≡3 (mod 7)` impossible?

**Answers:** (1) The residues are 0,1,2,4; the roots of 2 are 3,4. (2) The second giant value matches baby g²=2, giving x=5. (3) The subgroup generated by 2 is {1,2,4}; it excludes 3. A real logarithm cannot manufacture a valid integer exponent.
